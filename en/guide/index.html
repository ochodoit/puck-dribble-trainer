How to train hockey stickhandling at home

Puck Dribble Trainer is a free web game that uses the camera on your phone, iPad or laptop to track your puck and stick, so you can train ice hockey stickhandling at home. There is no app to install: it runs in the browser. A getting-started guide for players, parents and coaches. Get the camera position and the puck color right and tracking usually just works.

What you need

  • A device with a camera — an iPad or tablet, a phone or a laptop webcam.
  • A stand — anything that holds the device at least 80 cm high, looking down at the floor (a tripod, a chair or a shelf).
  • A floor — a white shooting pad or a plain, light-colored mat works best. 1–2 m wide is enough.
  • A puck — use a puck (or ball) whose color clearly differs from the floor. The more it stands out, the more accurately it is tracked. Black, orange or red on a white floor; white or neon on a dark mat.
  • A hockey stick — an indoor stick or a roller blade is ideal. Wrap the blade in tape whose color differs from both the puck and the floor (orange, blue) and the stick is tracked too.

Camera position (the most important part)

The more the camera looks down at the floor at an angle, the more accurate the puck position. On the same 2 m × 1.5 m floor, changing only the camera height gave a position error of about 2 cm at 1.6 m, about 3 cm at 1.2 m and about 6 cm at 80 cm; at 50 cm and below the puck started to get lost on the far side.

  • 1–1.5 m high is best. Keep it at least 80 cm high.
  • Tilt it down so the floor area fills at least a third of the screen height. Make the area as large as possible while all four corners stay on screen.
  • Too low makes tracking hard. With the device on the floor (50 cm or lower) the far side of the floor looks squashed flat, the puck shrinks to a few pixels, gets lost or is off by more than 10 cm on the far side, and stick tracking becomes unreliable.
  • Laptop webcams cannot tilt down much, so put the laptop on a chair or a box to get the camera at least 70 cm high.
  • If you move the camera or change its angle, fit the four corners again with the Area button. Perspective (the far side looking narrower) is corrected automatically when you fit the area.
  • Avoid shooting toward a bright window, and light the whole floor evenly.

Devices and big screens (best first)

The camera works best 1–1.5 m high, looking down at the floor at an angle, and the screen works best straight ahead at eye level, where you see it when you lift your head. The more you can place the camera and the screen separately, and the more you connect by cable (no screen delay), the better it plays.

  1. Laptop (PC) + USB webcam + HDMI to a TV or monitor (best) — the webcam stands on its own tripod or shelf at 1–1.5 m, so tracking is the most accurate, and the cable means almost no delay. Plug the webcam into USB (with a USB extension if needed) and connect the laptop to the TV with an HDMI cable (a USB-C to HDMI adapter on a Mac). On Windows press Win+P and choose Duplicate; on a Mac choose Mirror in display settings. Pick the USB webcam in Settings > Camera. F toggles full screen.
  2. Phone or iPad + HDMI cable to a TV — phone cameras are sharp and the cable keeps the delay low. iPhone and iPad use the Lightning Digital AV Adapter (Lightning models) or a USB-C to HDMI adapter (USB-C models, iPhone 15 and later). Android works with a USB-C to HDMI adapter only on phones with video output (some Samsung Galaxy S, Note and Tab S models, for example — look for “video output” or “DeX” in the manual). Use a 3–5 m HDMI cable and an adapter with a charging port so the phone can sit 1–1.5 m high.
  3. Phone or iPad + wireless mirroring to a TV — no cables, so it is easy to place, but the picture may lag 0.1–0.3 s, which you can feel in Reaction Target and Arcade. On iPhone or iPad open Control Center > Screen Mirroring and pick an Apple TV or an AirPlay TV (recent Samsung, LG and others); a Mac (macOS Monterey or later) can receive it too. On Android use Smart View (Samsung) or Cast screen (Chromecast, Google TV, Miracast TVs) in Quick Settings; a Windows laptop can receive it with “Projecting to this PC” (Wireless Display). Keep both on the same Wi-Fi, 5 GHz if possible.
  4. iPad or tablet on its own — the screen is big enough without connecting anything. Put it on a stand at least 1 m high with the front camera.
  5. Laptop built-in camera (+ HDMI to a TV) — works with no extra gear, but built-in cameras can barely tilt down and have lower quality, so tracking is the hardest. Put the laptop on a chair or a box so the camera is at least 70 cm high and tilt the screen back so the floor looks large. A separate USB webcam, as in option 1, is recommended if you can.
  6. Phone on its own — the screen is small to watch while playing, but fine for setting up and short practice. Turn it sideways.
  • Rear camera — when you watch a TV, you can use the sharper rear camera of the phone (the camera switch button at the top right). If left and right feel swapped, turn on “Mirror left/right” in Settings.
  • Keep it charging — the camera plus screen mirroring drains the battery fast. The app keeps the screen awake (in supported browsers), but low power mode may still turn it off, so play while charging.
  • Game mode on the TV — turn on the game mode in the TV picture settings to cut the display delay. Sound plays through the TV as well.
  • If you move the camera or the screen, fit the four corners again with the Area button.

First setup (5 minutes)

  1. Stand the device up — 1–1.5 m high, looking down at the floor at an angle. With the front camera you can watch the screen while you play.
  2. Start the camera — press “Start camera” (or “Start as guest”) at the top of the first screen and allow camera access.
  3. 1/3 Fit the area — drag the four corners onto your floor area. This also corrects the perspective when the far side looks narrower, and the saved area is reused next time.
  4. 2/3 Register the puck — put the puck (or ball) in the center circle (about twice the puck’s size) for 3 seconds and it learns its color and size.
  5. 3/3 Test move — take the puck to the four corner targets to check it is tracked everywhere. If the far targets keep losing it, raise the camera.
  6. Start a game — pick a game, time and difficulty, then hold the registered puck in the circle for 3 seconds to start hands-free. You can also turn your phone sideways and mirror it to a TV.

A 15-minute routine

3–4 short sessions a week work better than one long one.

  1. Wide Dribble, 3 min — warm up by reaching edge to edge.
  2. Figure 8, 3 min — switch between left/right first and clockwise/counterclockwise.
  3. Quick Hands, 2 min — short, fast touches inside the box.
  4. Reaction Target, 2 min — go the moment a target appears.
  5. Arcade or Dodge, 5 min — handle the puck while watching the whole screen.

Keep your head up

In a game you have to handle the puck without looking down at it. Raise “My puck transparency” in Settings step by step: your puck fades on screen, and at 100% you have to reach the targets by feel alone. Invisible mode on the mode screen is a separate challenge with its own leaderboard.

Tips for smooth play

  • The puck color must differ from the floor. A puck close to the floor color is tracked unreliably. A color that also differs from your skates and shoes is even better.
  • A black puck touching black skates looks like one blob. The app cuts the puck-shaped part out and keeps tracking it, but it can lose the puck if they stay together for more than 1.5 seconds or the puck is mostly under the skate.
  • Multi-colored balls (rainbow balls and the like) are not tracked well yet. Use a single-color puck or ball, or a two-tone puck where one color clearly differs from the floor, such as a white center with a black rim.
  • Keep objects of the same color as the puck (shoes, water bottles, bits of tape) out of the area.
  • When the stick covers the puck, the puck stays on screen, faded, for up to 1.5 seconds. Register the stick color in Calibrate too and a puck hidden right next to the blade follows the blade, which looks more natural.
  • While it is faded, no hits or points are counted, so you are never penalized for it.
  • Lifting the puck or sending it out of the area makes it disappear after a moment. Put it back in the area and it is picked up right away.
  • Young players should play away from furniture and glass, on a floor that is not slippery.

Calibration and settings help

  • Register the puck again — if you changed pucks or the light changed a lot, register it again with “3 s in circle” in Calibrate. The tolerance is set again automatically.
  • Set by tap — “Set by tap” in Calibrate shows the camera view instead of the game screen in every game, and pauses a game in progress. Tapping near the puck snaps to the nearest object. Press Esc to cancel.
  • Small puck — if the puck looks very small on screen, the detection resolution is raised to “Highest (400)” automatically when you register it.
  • Detection fine-tuning — open it in Settings for a live preview: red is what is detected as the puck, green as the stick. If only the puck is red, leave it. Raise the tolerance if the puck is only partly red; lower it if shoes or shadows turn red too. The preview stays inside the Settings window and never covers the game screen.
  • Settings from the mode window — the Settings button at the top right of the mode window opens Settings; closing it brings you back to the modes.
  • If the camera is blocked, allow it from the lock (camera) icon left of the address bar, then reload.
  • No white floor needed. Drag the area corners to the screen edges to play in front of a laptop webcam.
